# Breckwater Valley Robotics Club
## FY2026 Annual Program Report (July 1, 2025 to June 30, 2026)

Prepared for the Board of Directors by Tomas Vrba, Program Coordinator, with financials reviewed by Glenn Asheford, Treasurer.

### Who we are

Breckwater Valley Robotics Club is an after-school and weekend robotics program for teenagers in grades 9 through 12 who live in and around Breckwater, a farm town of about 2,400 people. We work out of a rented bay in the old Halvorsen implement dealership on Mill Road, which we have turned into a shop with four build tables, a parts wall, and a practice field marked out in floor tape. We serve students from three schools: Breckwater High School, Ferris Township School, and Calloway Consolidated. The nearest engineering program of any kind is 140 miles away. For most of our students, the club is the first time they have used a CAD program, written code that moves something, or been asked to present their own work to a panel of strangers.

The club has run since 2021. FY2026 was our fifth season and our largest.

### FY2026 by the numbers

- 64 students enrolled at the start of the season in September 2025.
- 52 students completed the full season, a completion rate of 81 percent.
- 23 of the 64 enrolled students were girls (36 percent), up from 14 the year before.
- 41 of the 64 qualified for free or reduced-price school meals.
- 9 competition teams built and ran robots across 6 regional events.
- 2 teams qualified for the state championship in April 2026; one finished 11th of 48.
- 1,180 optional shop hours were logged by completers across 38 weeks, about 23 hours per completer beyond required meetings.
- 17 adult volunteers gave an estimated 1,450 hours, mostly as drivers, judges at our home scrimmage, and mentors on Saturday build days.

Of the 12 students who did not finish, 7 left for jobs during harvest or calving season, 3 moved out of the area, and 2 left for reasons we did not learn. We now call every student who misses two weeks in a row, and we want to understand the work conflicts better before next fall.

### What the money paid for

Total FY2026 expenses were $148,600.

| Category | Amount |
|---|---|
| Staff: part-time coordinator and two coach stipends | $71,500 |
| Robot parts, kits, and tools | $28,400 |
| Competition travel: van rental, fuel, lodging, entry fees | $21,300 |
| Shop space and utilities | $12,000 |
| Insurance, accounting, and administration | $9,600 |
| Student meals and travel stipends | $5,800 |
| **Total** | **$148,600** |

Travel remains our hardest line to control. Every regional event is at least a two-hour drive, and state was a two-night trip. Because many of our families cannot cover a hotel room, the club pays for all lodging and meals on the road.

### Where the money came from

Total FY2026 revenue was $150,400, from five sources.

| Source | Amount | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Prairie Lantern Fund (second year of a three-year grant) | $42,000 | 28% |
| Harrow County Community Foundation | $35,000 | 23% |
| Individual donors and the spring pancake breakfast | $31,600 | 21% |
| Dunmore Grain Cooperative | $30,000 | 20% |
| Participant fees (sliding scale, $0 to $250) | $11,800 | 8% |
| **Total** | **$150,400** | **100%** |

We ended the year with a surplus of $1,800, which went to our operating reserve. The reserve now holds $14,200, a little over one month of expenses. Thirty-eight of our 64 students paid no fee at all.

No single funder provides more than 28 percent of our budget, and the board intends to keep it that way.

### Results, and what we can and cannot claim

Our clearest result is about what our graduating seniors did next. Eighteen seniors completed the FY2026 season. As of September 2026, 14 of them (78 percent) have enrolled in a two-year or four-year college, a technical school, or a registered apprenticeship. Nine of those 14 chose engineering, welding and fabrication, electrical, agricultural mechanics, or computer science.

We are proud of that number. We also want to be honest about its limits. It comes from what students and parents told us in a follow-up call, not from enrollment records. Eighteen students is a small group, and a few changed plans would move the percentage a long way. We have no comparison group: students who join a robotics club may already be the ones most likely to go on to school. The county figure we sometimes cite for context, that about 51 percent of local graduates enroll in postsecondary education, is from the 2023 graduating class and comes from a different source. We do not claim the club caused the difference. Starting this year, we are asking graduating seniors for permission to check their enrollment through the state's student records office so that next year's number is verified.

What we can say with more confidence is what happened inside the program. On our end-of-season survey, completed by 49 of the 52 completers, 44 said they felt more confident speaking in front of adults, and 40 said they had used a skill from the club in a class or a job. Our coaches saw it too. In September most students could not read a wiring diagram. By March, every team had at least two students who could rebuild their robot's drivetrain without help.

### In their words

Mireya, a junior at Calloway Consolidated, said this at our end-of-season dinner:

> "I used to think engineering was something that happened in cities. Now I fix the pivot on my uncle's sprayer and I know why it broke."

### What we learned

Three lessons from this year shaped next year's plan.

First, the ride is the program. Students at Ferris Township and Calloway live 25 to 40 minutes from the shop, and our single leased van could not cover both routes on build nights. On nights the van ran late, attendance from those schools dropped by about a third.

Second, more girls joined when we ran a Saturday build session led by our coach Priya Lindqvist. Several girls told us they came because a friend did and because they knew the session would be small.

Third, demand is ahead of us. We turned away 21 students in September 2025 because we did not have coaches or parts for more than nine teams.

### FY2027 plan and cost

In FY2027 we will add a fourth school, Ostrander Rural High School, grow from 9 to 12 teams, and serve about 85 students. To do that we need a second van, a third coach, and parts for three more teams.

Our FY2027 budget is $186,000. That is our current $148,600 operating cost plus $37,400 in new expansion costs:

| Expansion item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Second leased van, insurance, and fuel | $9,800 |
| Third coach stipend | $12,000 |
| Kits, parts, and tools for three new teams | $11,400 |
| Added competition travel and entry fees | $4,200 |
| **Expansion total** | **$37,400** |

We expect our five current funding sources to hold steady and cover the $148,600 base budget. We are seeking a new partner to fund the full $37,400 expansion for one year, after which we will fold the costs into our base budget and grow our fee and donor revenue to carry them.

### Thank you

This season ran on volunteer drivers, a landlord who charges us half rent, and families who show up at a pancake breakfast every spring. Thank you to every one of them.
