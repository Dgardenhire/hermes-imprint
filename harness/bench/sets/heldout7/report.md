# Bramblecote Wildlife Recovery
## FY2026 Annual Program Report
Fiscal year July 1, 2025 to June 30, 2026
Ketterly, in the Harrow Valley

---

## Who we are

Bramblecote Wildlife Recovery is a small licensed rehabilitation center on four acres off Mill Lane, two miles north of downtown Ketterly. We take in injured, sick, and orphaned native wildlife from across Ketterly County, treat them, and return as many as we can to the wild. We also run a 24-hour wildlife hotline and a school program that teaches children what to do, and what not to do, when they find an animal in trouble.

We have 2.5 full-time care staff, a contract veterinarian who is on site two mornings a week, and a volunteer corps that did most of the feeding, cleaning, and transport this year.

## The year in numbers

Demand kept climbing for the third straight year. More residents know we exist, and the county's new road along the Harrow River corridor has added vehicle strikes.

| Measure | FY2024 | FY2025 | FY2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Animals admitted | 1,142 | 1,386 | 1,611 |
| Species treated | 96 | 104 | 117 |
| Released to the wild | 548 (48%) | 707 (51%) | 870 (54%) |
| Hotline calls answered | 3,020 | 3,710 | 4,480 |
| Animals kept out of intake by hotline coaching | 410 | 498 | 612 |
| Active volunteers | 64 | 81 | 97 |
| Volunteer hours | 7,380 | 9,150 | 11,240 |
| School and library visits | 22 | 31 | 38 |
| Students reached | 1,240 | 1,790 | 2,150 |

Admissions rose 41 percent over two years, from 1,142 to 1,611. Songbirds made up 46 percent of FY2026 intake, mammals 38 percent, reptiles and amphibians 9 percent, and raptors 7 percent (113 birds, mostly red-tailed hawks, barred owls, and American kestrels).

The hotline number we are proudest of is the one that does not show up in our cages. In FY2026, staff and trained volunteers talked 612 callers through leaving a healthy animal where it was: a fledgling robin hopping on a lawn, a fawn bedded in tall grass with its mother nearby, a baby rabbit nest uncovered by a mower and then covered back up. Every one of those animals stayed with its parents, and we did not spend a dollar on its food or a cage-day on its care.

## The result we report, and its limits

Our release rate rose from 48 percent in FY2024 to 54 percent in FY2026. That is the headline number most rehabilitation centers lead with, and it did go up.

We want to be plain about why. Part of the rise comes from what came through the door, not from better care. Orphaned juvenile songbirds and mammals, which tend to be healthy and release at high rates, grew faster than adult trauma cases such as vehicle strikes and window collisions, which release at much lower rates. When our contract veterinarian, Dr. Ilse Carrow, adjusted FY2026 results to match the FY2024 mix of species and injury types, the release rate was about 49 percent, roughly one point better than two years ago rather than six.

We also cannot yet say how released animals fare afterward. We do not track survival after release. A one-point gain in a year when admissions rose 41 percent and staffing did not change is still a real result. It is just a smaller result than the raw number suggests, and we would rather a funder hear that from us.

## What did not go well

Raptors are our weakest link. We have no flight enclosure large enough for a hawk or owl to rebuild flight muscle before release. In FY2026 we transferred 41 of our 113 raptors to Crane Ridge Raptor Center, two hours away, for final conditioning. Each transfer costs a volunteer most of a day, stresses the bird, and takes it out of our hands at the stage where it is closest to going home. Crane Ridge has told us it will cap the transfers it accepts from outside its region starting in 2027.

We also lost two long-time evening volunteers in the spring, and for six weeks the overnight hotline rolled to a staff member's personal phone. We have since trained four new overnight volunteers.

## FY2027 plan: the raptor flight enclosure

Our priority for FY2027 is a 100-foot curved flight enclosure, so that hawks and owls can complete their recovery at Bramblecote and be released in the county where they were found.
